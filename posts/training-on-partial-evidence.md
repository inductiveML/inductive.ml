Hiding some of the examples during training made a small program-writing model better at inventing programs for combinations it had never seen: from 54% to 73% on our held-out test. On a model with no hand-built features, hiding reaches about 70% within 10,000 training steps, while training on every example stays near 59%. With longer training the gain is no longer clear, and simply dropping training rows does at least as well. So the effect is real but limited, and we can't yet separate it from training on fewer rows.

## The setup

Abacus is a toy world small enough to check everything exactly. A program is 6 slots, each filled with one of 7 operations. Together those make 117,649 programs, which compute 2,143 distinct functions. The program moves a pointer along a tape of numbers and carries one value in a register, and it runs its 6 slots once per tape cell.

Each function is shown through example tapes, 8 at each of the lengths 4 to 8, 40 in total.

The "writer" is a small neural network that reads those examples and proposes programs, keeping its 8 or 64 best guesses. A guess counts as correct if it computes the right function at every length, including lengths 16, 32 and 64, which never appear in the examples.

The test is invention. We held one pairing of building blocks out of training: at length 8, output cell 2 reads input cell 4 and output cell 5 reads input cell 7. Training includes functions with either read alone, but never both.

We give the writer 20 fresh sets of examples per target and ask how often the right function is among its 64 best guesses. We call that held-out accuracy. Every hiding result below averages 32 writers that differ only in starting weights.

## The writer can invent

The writer does produce correct programs for combinations it never trained on. In one check we took the 12 solved targets where no training program contained both reads. On all 12, the best writer found the right function on 100% of fresh examples, and on 0% of examples from unrelated functions. So these answers came from the examples, not from a program the writer had memorized.

Which targets a writer solves isn't just luck. Swapping two batches halfway through training left the writer's solved targets almost unchanged (similarity 0.97). Writers sharing starting weights agreed more (0.71) than writers sharing a data order (0.64) or neither (0.61). Starting weights were the only one of the two with a clear effect, but they don't settle the answer alone: the same weights with a new data order still gave a noticeably different set.

Two checks shaped the reading:

- **Every kind of evidence must stay present.** Hiding everything but the short lengths on every row collapsed accuracy to 0.195. Keeping a few tapes at every length gave the best result.
- **Fewer full rows may explain part of it.** Giving half the rows zero weight instead of hiding their examples moved accuracy by only 0.8 points. At 75%, though, hiding (0.666) beat dropping (0.580) by 8.5 points with an interval of −0.5 to +17.9, so that gap isn't clear either.

## Without hand-built features

The writer above reads 576 hand-built yes/no features per length, such as "output cell i equals input cell j plus k". Those features could be doing the real work, so we also trained a writer of 1,363,335 parameters that reads raw tapes.

Our first attempt went badly. Hiding on 75% of rows cut the raw writer's accuracy from 0.585 to 0.174 at 40,000 steps. Both writers had also been given a current program as input, and that input alone fixed the answer for 92.8% of training rows. In one retrained writer, the first guess was exactly right on 95.1% of training rows, but in the right function on only 7.3% of new input programs for the same functions.

So we removed the program input and hid examples at every length. Those were two changes at once, so this doesn't show which one mattered. Removing the input alone cost nothing on full-example training (0.596 without it, 0.585 with it).

Full-example training didn't catch up within our budgets. It stayed near 0.59 from 10,000 to 40,000 steps. What caught up was dropping 75% of rows: 0.506, then 0.685, then 0.714, which is clearly above full-example training at 40,000 steps. A probe found no sign that the raw writer memorized training rows: it recovered exact labels equally well from stored and fresh tapes.

Our reading is that hiding gets a raw writer to about 0.70 sooner, in 10,000 steps where dropping rows needs 20,000 to 40,000. We can't say from these runs what happens past 40,000 steps, or whether hiding adds anything beyond training on fewer rows.

## What didn't work

- **An exact memory inside the writer.** Adding a lookup store did not help: 44 of 288 test cases solved at 8 guesses, the same as the plain writer.
- **Picking well under ambiguity.** When writers trained with hidden examples saw only one or eight length-4 tapes, several functions fit. On held-out targets the pool's pick was right 6.2% of the time with eight tapes and 6.0% with one, below a random pick among the fitting functions (28.9% and 20.0%). The right function was often among its candidates (62.1% and 42.0%), but it wasn't the one picked.
- **Giving the writer the current program.** The raw writer used it to fit training pairs instead of learning a rule that carried over to new programs.
- **Removing whole lengths of evidence.** Training only on short-length examples collapsed held-out accuracy.

## Caveats

- **One world, one split, 24 targets.** All the hiding results use one toy language and one held-out pairing. A second pairing we tried earlier was much harder: the plain writer solved 4 of 144 test cases there at 8 guesses, against 40 of 144 on this one. Running hiding on it would be the cheapest way to sharpen these numbers.
- **Not separated from smaller effective batches.** On the raw writer, dropping rows was at least as good as hiding by 40,000 steps, so part of the gain may be the known benefit of training on fewer rows per step.
- **Small models.** The largest writer has 1.4 million parameters. Nothing here was tested at language-model scale.

## Applying it to language models

The idea that might transfer is training on partial demonstrations: show the model less of the evidence on some examples, while still asking for the full answer. We haven't tested this on a language model. These are three hypotheses from the toy world:

1. **Hide evidence, never the target.** The answer stays complete; only the inputs that justify it are thinned.
2. **Keep every kind of evidence represented.** Thin each kind, and never drop a kind entirely.
3. **Remove lookup keys.** Anything that lets the model match an input to a memorized answer, like the program input here, should go, and demonstrations should be fresh.

The best fits are probably fine-tuning on tasks where the model infers a rule from examples, and distillation where a teacher sees more than the student. Pretraining is the least clear fit.

The measure to use is compute to reach a target accuracy on held-out combinations, compared against both full training and simply dropping rows, since that is where the effect showed up.
